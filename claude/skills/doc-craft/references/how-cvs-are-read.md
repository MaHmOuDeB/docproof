# How a CV Actually Gets Read

## The packaging theory

A supermarket shelf holds twenty brands of baking soda, all able to do the job. The shopper
doesn't read the labels. They scan until one package says, clearly and fast, "this is what you
need."

Recruiters shop the same way. At junior-to-mid level most applicants are qualified on paper;
that's the baseline, not the differentiator. The CV that gets picked is the one that
communicates fit **fastest and most clearly**.

> The question is not "can this person do the job?"
> It is "does this page prove it in eight seconds?"

## The three phases

### Phase 1: ATS (parse, then match)

1. **Parsing.** The system extracts the document into fields (name, titles, employers, dates,
   skills, education) before it looks at a job description. Multi-column layouts, tables, text
   boxes, graphics and content in page headers/footers can scramble or drop here, however good
   the content is.
2. **Matching.** Parsed fields are compared against the posting and the recruiter's filters.
   Some systems match semantically; many match literally. **When in doubt, use the ad's exact
   phrase**: "stakeholder communication", not "talking to clients".

**Myth check:** the widely repeated "75% of CVs are auto-rejected by ATS" traces back to an
undocumented marketing claim. Most applications get human eyes; where ATS does auto-reject, it's
usually a hard knockout (work authorisation, required degree, years, language). Silence after
applying is mostly a volume problem, not "the robot binned me."

### Phase 2: the 6–10 second human scan

What the eye lands on, in order:
1. A title line that matches the role
2. Recognisable job titles and company names
3. Relevant keywords and skills
4. **Numbers**: any quantified result catches the eye
5. A clean, easy layout

If none of these land, the reader moves on. They have 200 CVs.

### Phase 3: the detailed read

Only now does writing quality matter: bullet strength, relevance, credibility, coherence. This
is where overclaiming gets caught and where specific, verifiable detail wins.

## Four questions to answer in under ten seconds

| Question | Answered by |
|---|---|
| Who are you? | Title line + first sentence of the summary (title, level, domain) |
| What can you do? | Skills section, grouped, matched to the ad |
| What have you achieved? | Bullets with verbs, scope and results |
| Are you right for this role? | The visible connection between all of the above and the ad |

## Match × simplicity

A winning CV is a function of two variables, and the relationship is **multiplicative**:

- **Match** rises with the number of genuinely relevant keywords a recruiter can tick off.
- **Simplicity** falls with the time it takes them to tick them off.

Zero on either side is zero overall.

## Should the candidate apply at all? The 70% rule

Applying to the wrong job is the fastest way to get rejected. Career-guidance sources converge:

- **~70–75% of the listed must-haves** genuinely met: apply. `docproof match` reports this
  coverage figure from the fact base.
- "Met" means real work in it, not interest.
- One missing year of experience is rarely fatal if everything else fits; be ready to argue why
  the candidate is ready now.
- An explicitly **optional** language is a green light; a **required** C1 in a language the
  candidate doesn't have is a knockout.
- "Spray and pray" (the same CV to 50 companies) hurts the candidate's reputation with repeat
  employers and hurts morale. Repeated poor-fit applications to one company can bury a later
  good-fit one.

Below the threshold the candidate can still apply as a deliberate long shot. Label it that way,
so a predictable rejection doesn't read as a verdict on them.
